What Credit Score Do You Need for a DSCR Loan?

DSCR loans start at a 620 credit score. See how your score changes rate and leverage, which score lenders use, and how to raise it before you apply.

Key Partners Funding8 min readRental investors checking whether their credit qualifies

Our DSCR program requires a minimum credit score of 620. Above that line, your score does not decide whether you can get a DSCR loan. It decides what the loan costs and how much of the property's value you can borrow. A DSCR loan skips tax returns, pay stubs and debt to income math, but it does not skip the credit report. The lender pulls credit on the person guaranteeing the loan, and the score feeds straight into the rate, the maximum loan to value, and how much flexibility you get when the rent is thin. Here is how lenders use the number, which of your scores they count, and what to do in the 60 days before you apply.

What the score does on a loan that ignores your income

With no income to analyze, a DSCR lender is left with three ways to judge risk: the property's ratio of rent to payment, the amount of equity in the deal, and the borrower's record of paying debts. Credit is the third. It affects a file in three ways.

  • Pricing. A higher score earns a lower rate, or the same rate with fewer points.
  • Leverage. The top loan to value is usually reserved for stronger scores. On our program the ceiling is 80 percent, and a lower score usually means more money down.
  • Flexibility. A strong score can offset a ratio that sits close to 1.0. A weak score paired with a weak ratio is the hardest file to approve.

How lenders tier credit scores

Across the DSCR market, rate sheets are built as grids. Credit score runs down one side, usually in bands of about 20 points, and loan to value runs across the top. Where your file lands on the grid sets the starting price, and adjustments for the ratio, the loan size, the property type and the prepayment option are layered on from there.

Two things follow from that structure.

First, a few points can matter a great deal or not at all. Moving from 698 to 702 may cross a band and improve the price. Moving from 702 to 715 may change nothing. If you know you are just under a band, a small improvement before you apply is worth the effort.

Second, score and down payment trade against each other. The penalty for a lower score is usually steepest at the highest loan to value and smaller at 70 or 65 percent. An investor with a 660 score may get a better deal by putting 25 percent down than by stretching to 80.

We are not printing a grid here, because the size of each step changes with the market and a stale table would do more harm than good. Our page on how DSCR rates are priced explains each factor, and a real quote on a real property will show you exactly where you land.

Which score counts

The number on your banking or credit card app is probably not the one a lender will see. Three differences explain the gap.

  • The scoring model. Mortgage lenders use specific FICO versions built for mortgage lending. Many free apps show a VantageScore or a newer FICO model. The same report can produce noticeably different numbers under different models.
  • Three bureaus. The lender pulls a report from all three bureaus and gets three scores. The usual rule is to use the middle one. If only two scores come back, the lower one is used.
  • More than one guarantor. When two people guarantee the loan, lenders differ. Some use the lower of the two middle scores, and some use the score of the primary guarantor. Ask which rule applies before you decide who signs.

The practical step is to ask what your qualifying score is early in the process, before you have paid for an appraisal.

What else on the report matters

The score is a summary. Underwriters also read the report behind it, and a few items carry weight of their own.

  • Mortgage payment history. A late mortgage payment in the last year or two matters more than a late credit card payment. A DSCR lender is making a mortgage, and how you have paid mortgages is the closest evidence available.
  • Major credit events. A bankruptcy, foreclosure, short sale or deed in lieu usually carries a waiting period measured in years. The length varies by lender and by event.
  • Depth of credit. A report with only one or two accounts, or accounts that are all new, can be a problem even when the score looks fine. Lenders like to see several accounts with some history.
  • Open collections, judgments and tax liens. These generally need to be paid or resolved before closing, particularly anything that could attach to the property.

What the underwriter does not do is add up your monthly debts and compare them to your income. There is no debt to income ratio on a DSCR loan. High balances still matter, but only through their effect on your score.

The LLC does not replace your credit

Every DSCR loan on our program closes in an LLC. That sometimes leads investors to assume their personal credit is out of the picture. It is not. A new LLC has no credit history of its own, so the lender underwrites the person or people behind it, and those individuals sign a personal guarantee. Our article on why DSCR lenders require an LLC covers how the entity and the guarantee fit together.

Will the loan show up on your personal credit?

There are two parts to this.

The inquiry will. Applying involves a hard credit pull on each guarantor. Scoring models generally treat several mortgage inquiries made within a short shopping window as a single inquiry, so comparing two or three lenders over a couple of weeks does little harm.

The loan itself often will not. Because the borrower is the LLC, many DSCR lenders and servicers do not report the monthly payment history to the personal credit bureaus. That keeps the debt off your personal report, which helps investors who are also applying for conventional financing elsewhere. Practices vary, so ask. And remember that the guarantee is real. A default can still reach you personally.

How score, ratio and down payment work together

No single number makes or breaks a DSCR file. Think of three dials, where strength on one can make up for softness on another.

  • Strong score, thin ratio. A 760 score with a ratio of 1.02 at 80 percent loan to value is a file most lenders will work with, at a price that reflects the thin ratio.
  • Modest score, strong deal. A 650 score with a ratio of 1.35 and 30 percent down is also a workable file. The property is carrying the weight.
  • Soft on all three. A 630 score, a ratio of 1.0 and the minimum down payment is where approvals get difficult and pricing gets expensive.

If your score is the weak dial, the levers you control fastest are the down payment and the property you choose. Test a few scenarios in our DSCR calculator to see how a larger down payment changes the ratio.

Raising your score in the 60 days before you apply

If you are close to a band, or close to 620, a few steps can move the number in a short time.

  1. Pay down revolving balances. Credit utilization is the fastest lever. Getting each card under about 30 percent of its limit helps, and lower is better.
  2. Pay before the statement date. Card issuers usually report the balance on the statement date, not the due date. Paying a few days before the statement closes lowers the balance the bureaus see.
  3. Do not open new accounts. A new card or car loan adds an inquiry and lowers the average age of your accounts.
  4. Do not close old cards. Closing a card removes its limit from your utilization math.
  5. Check all three reports for errors. A wrong late payment or an account that is not yours can be disputed. Start early, because disputes take time.
  6. Ask about a rapid rescore. If you have paid balances down and need the new score quickly, ask your lender whether an expedited update through their credit vendor is available.

Business credit cards deserve a mention. Many do not report to personal bureaus, so moving business spending onto a true business card can lower the utilization on your personal report over time.

If you are below 620

We cannot make a DSCR loan below the minimum, and we would rather say so up front. The realistic options are:

  • Take a few months to rebuild. For many borrowers within 20 or 30 points of the line, paying down balances and correcting errors is enough.
  • Add a partner with stronger credit. The partner must be an actual owner of the LLC and must guarantee the loan. Agree on ownership and responsibilities in writing first.
  • Keep building the rest of the file. Cash for a larger down payment and a property with a strong ratio will both help once the score is there.

What to do next

Know your real qualifying score, know your ratio, and know how much you can put down. With those three numbers a lender can tell you in one conversation where you stand.
